UNITED STATES PATENT OFFICE.

PETER COOPER HEWITT, OF NEW YORK, N. Y., ASSIGNOR TO PETER COOPER HEWITT, TRUSTEE, OF SAME PLACE.

ELECTRIC LAMP.

SPECIFICATION forming part of Letters Patent No. 682,690, dated September 17, 1901.
Application filed April 5, 1900. Serial No. 11,605. (No model.)

To all whom it may concern:
Be it known that I, PETER COOPER HEWITT, a citizen of the United States, residing at New York, in the county of New York and State of New York, have invented certain new and useful Improvements in Electric Lamps, of which the following is a specification.

My invention relates to that class of electric lamps in which light is produced by the passage of an electric current through a vapor or gas, and the object of the invention is to provide a method of and apparatus for producing light in this manner with high efficiency and under conditions which will permit of commercial application.

Heretofore in devices of this character—such as Geissler tubes and vacuum spark discharges—the currents employed have been of very high potential (thousands of volts) and of small quantity (fractions of a milliampere), produced by induction-coils, static machines, or high-potential transformers. Such devices have not been capable of commercial use for general illumination, owing to the dangerous potentials required, the small amount of light produced, and the great cost and inefficiency of the apparatus.

I have discovered that when an exhausted tube containing a vaporizable conducting substance, such as mercury, is connected in circuit with a source of electric current of moderate electromotive force—such as is ordinary used in commercial incandescent or arc lighting systems (say, fifty to one hundred and twenty volts)—the tube offers a very high initial resistance to the passage of the current, which resistance resides largely at the surface of the negative electrode or cathode. To start the lamp I apply a momentary higher potential of several thousand volts to break down this initial cathode resistance. The moment this cathode resistance is broken down, the electrical resistance of the vapor column collapses and the tube continues to operate smoothly from the source of moderate potential, emitting a brilliant and highly efficient light.

The resistance of the vapor column itself depends upon its length, cross-section, density, and temperature. Under normal operating conditions, as the current increases, the temperature and density of the vapor increase, but the conductivity of the vapor also increases at a rate such that the difference of potential between the terminals of the lamp remains substantially constant or even decreases with increments of current (negative differential resistance). To stabilize the operation of the lamp, a steadying resistance or inductive ballast is placed in series with the lamp.

Referring to Fig. 1, 1 is an exhausted transparent tube of glass or other suitable material, having a liquid electrode 2 (preferably mercury) at its lower end, and a solid electrode 3 (preferably iron or graphite) at its upper end. Leading-in wires 4 and 5 are sealed through the glass walls to connect with the respective electrodes. Surrounding the upper electrode 3 is an enlarged cooling or condensing chamber 8, which provides radiating surface to condense excess mercury vapor and return the liquid mercury down the tube to the cathode 2.

If the temperature of the lamp is allowed to become too high, the vapor pressure increases to such an extent that the lamp is liable to extinguish itself. The condensing chamber 8 is therefore constructed with sufficient radiating surface to dissipate the heat and maintain the vapor density at an operative value.

To start the lamp, I have found it advantageous to apply an initial high-potential impulse across the electrodes. This may be accomplished by various circuit arrangements. In the arrangement shown in Fig. 3, a source of moderate potential 10 is connected to the lamp through a transformer secondary 11 and an inductance coil 12. A primary circuit 13 includes a circuit-interrupter 14. When the interrupter is operated, a high electromotive force is induced in the secondary 11, which breaks down the cold cathode resistance of the lamp and permits the main current from source 10 to flow continuously through the vapor path.

To facilitate starting and prevent localized static strain on the glass, a conducting band or coating 15 is placed around the exterior of the tube near the cathode 2, and is electrically connected with the opposite electrode 3 or the supply circuit. This conducting band acts as a static dissipator and condenser plate, concentrating the electric field at the meniscus of the mercury cathode and lowering the voltage required to initiate the cathode spot.

In the automatic starting system shown in Fig. 4, the transformer primary 20 is connected across the supply mains 14, 14 through an automatic magnetic interrupter 21 and a series cutout switch 22. The secondary 23 is in series with the lamp 1 and ballast 24. Upon closing the main switch, current flows through the primary circuit, causing the interrupter 21 to vibrate rapidly and generate high-voltage pulses in secondary 23. These pulses strike across the lamp tube 1, establishing the vapor arc.

The moment the lamp arc is struck, the operating current traversing the lamp energizes the solenoid of cutout switch 22, pulling its core and opening the primary circuit. The high-voltage starting mechanism is thus automatically disconnected, and the lamp continues to run quietly from the moderate-voltage supply lines.

In the manufacture of the lamp, the tube is exhausted to a high degree of vacuum by a mercury pump while being heated in an oven or by burners to drive off occluded gases from the glass walls and electrodes. A small quantity of starting material, such as sulfide of mercury or red oxide of mercury, may be introduced into the tube before sealing to assist in the initial breakdown.

The electrode 3 at the upper end of the tube is preferably made of iron, steel, or graphite, which are not readily vaporized and do not give off occluded gases or carbon particles to blacken the glass. The liquid mercury cathode 2 at the bottom of the tube is continuously replenished by the condensed mercury trickling down from the chamber 8, so that the cathode is virtually indestructible and does not deteriorate with use.

The color of the light produced by the mercury arc is a characteristic greenish-blue, extremely rich in actinic and visual rays of high luminous efficiency. For photographic work, printing, and general industrial illumination, this light is exceptionally valuable, while its electrical efficiency is many times greater than that of incandescent carbon lamps.

Where alternating currents are employed to operate the lamp, both electrodes may consist of mercury pools, or suitable rectifying connections may be provided.
